Analysis at Advanced Higher

Analysis is the process of understanding exactly what the client and end users want from the system being developed — before any design work starts. At Higher you identified the purpose, scope, boundaries and functional requirements of a problem. At Advanced Higher, analysis grows into a full requirements specification, supported by research (feasibility studies and user surveys), planning (scheduling, resources and Gantt charts) and a new design notation — the UML use case diagram.

This content applies equally to SDD, DDD and WDD problems, and to problems that integrate them — which is exactly what your project requires. Analysis is worth 10 marks of the project and between 3 and 8 marks of the question paper.

Key Terms

Requirements Specification
The document produced at the end of analysis — end-user requirements, scope, boundaries, constraints and functional requirements. Often forms the basis of a legally binding contract.
End-User Requirements
What the people using the solution expect it to let them do.
Scope
What the project must cover, including its deliverables.
Boundaries
What the project will not cover — the limits of the development.
Constraints
Restrictions on the development — e.g. the language, platform, budget or timescale that must be used.
Functional Requirements
What the solution must do, defined in terms of inputs, processes and outputs.
Feasibility Study
Research to determine whether the project can actually be achieved — economically, technically, legally and in the time available.
Use Case Diagram
A UML diagram capturing the dynamic behaviour of a system: actors, use cases and the relationships between them.

The Requirements Specification

The requirements specification pulls the whole of analysis together into one document. It details the scope, boundaries and constraints of the intended solution, and sets out how the software will be designed, tested and evaluated before hand-over. Because the client agrees to pay for what it describes, it is often the basis of a legally binding contract between the client and the developer — a favourite exam fact.

Purpose and functional requirements

As at Higher, the purpose is a plain-language statement of what the solution will do, and the functional requirements are defined in terms of:

  • Inputs — the data the solution receives (from the user, a file, a form or a database);
  • Processes — what is done with that data (each process often becoming a separate procedure, function or query);
  • Outputs — the results displayed, stored or confirmed to the user.
Example — functional requirements for a stock search program (SDD)

Inputs: itemID, price and number in stock, read from a sequential file; an itemID entered by the end user.

Processes: read the file data into a 2D array; sort the data in order of itemID (low to high); search the 2D array for the itemID entered by the end user.

Outputs: if a match is found, display the itemID, price and number in stock; if not, display a suitable "not found" message.

Notice how the processes name Advanced Higher concepts (2D array, sort, search) — in your project, four of your functional requirements must relate to Advanced Higher concepts in exactly this way.

Scope and boundaries

Scope and boundaries are opposite sides of the same coin. Between them they give a precise description of the extent of the project:

  • the scope clarifies what the project must cover — including its deliverables (e.g. a detailed design, a test plan with completed test table, a working program, the results of testing and an evaluation report);
  • the boundaries clarify what the project will not cover (e.g. "the program will read data from a sequential file — since the data is accurate, input validation is not required").

Constraints

Constraints are new at Advanced Higher. They are the restrictions that apply to the development, limiting the design decisions that can be made. The main project-management constraints are time, scope and cost, but others include:

TypeExamples
Technical constraintsKnowledge or availability of development tools and programming languages; the operating system or platform the solution must run on; hardware capacity; performance requirements.
Business constraintsDeadlines and timescales that must be met; the available budget; the make-up of the development team; software licensing restrictions.
Further constraintsEconomic considerations; political issues.
💡
Exam technique: a constraint is always phrased as a restriction on the development, not a feature of the solution. "The program must be written in Python" is a constraint; "the program must validate input" is a functional requirement.

Research — Feasibility Studies & User Surveys

Before committing time and money, a development team carries out a feasibility study: is the proposed expenditure of time and money likely to be worthwhile, and can the project's objectives actually be achieved? You need to be able to describe, exemplify and implement research for four types of feasibility, plus user surveys.

The four types of feasibility

TypeThe question it answersExample issue
EconomicCan the project be completed within the available budget — and is the cost justified by the benefit? A cost-benefit analysis compares the costs of acquiring, installing and maintaining the system against tangible benefits such as reduced running costs and increased throughput.The project cannot afford to hire the extra developers needed to finish on time.
TimeCan the project be delivered within the required timescale, with the right people and resources available when needed? (Sometimes called schedule feasibility.)The system is needed for the new financial year but development would take 14 months.
LegalCan the solution be created and operated without breaking the law — data protection (GDPR), health and safety, and software licensing?A system that profiles customers' spending habits would breach data-protection law.
TechnicalDoes the technology needed for the solution exist, and does the organisation have (or can it acquire) the necessary hardware, software and expertise?The software needed to link two data sources into the project is not available.

User surveys

The users of an existing system are the best people to identify its problems and how it could be improved. When the opinion of many users is needed, a survey or questionnaire captures the information the project team requires. Even for a brand-new system, surveying the target user group reveals how they currently carry out the process the software will replace — for example, interviewing librarians before replacing a card-index system.

Planning — Scheduling, Resources & Gantt Charts

Project management is the process of planning and controlling the activities of a project so that it is delivered on time, with the required features, within budget, to the required quality. A project plan is more than a list of tasks — it details:

  • Scheduling — when each task happens, in what order, and which tasks depend on others finishing first;
  • Resources — the people, hardware and software available to complete the work, and who is responsible for each task;
  • Milestones — the key points at which progress is measured.

Gantt charts

A Gantt chart is like a horizontal bar graph used to plan and schedule a project involving several concurrent tasks. The horizontal axis represents the time scale; each bar shows the start and finish of one task. Its big advantage is that it shows, at a glance, the progress of the whole project — including which tasks overlap and which cannot start until another finishes.

UML Use Case Diagrams

Unified Modelling Language (UML) provides a standard way to visualise the analysis and design of a software system. To model a system fully we need to capture its dynamic behaviour — what happens when the system is running — and that is the job of the use case diagram. (Its partner, the class diagram, captures the static structure and is covered in SDD Design.)

A use case diagram is used to gather the requirements of the system, get an outside view of it, and aid communication between the client and the developer. It has four components:

1 — System boundary

A rectangle that represents the limits of the system being developed. Every use case sits inside the boundary; every actor sits outside it.

2 — Actors

An actor is a person or external entity (another system, a server, even a scheduled timer) that interacts with the system. Actors are drawn as stick figures with the actor type written underneath, and each actor must be associated with at least one use case.

  • A primary actor uses the system to achieve a goal (e.g. a customer buying an item) — placed on the left of the system boundary.
  • A secondary actor supports the system in delivering that goal (e.g. the bank that processes the payment) — placed on the right.

3 — Use cases

A use case is an action or sequence of actions that the system must perform — drawn as an ellipse with the name written inside. The name should describe an observable, useful result for an actor: Place Order, Manage Account, Update Subscription.

4 — Relationships

Five types of relationship can appear on a use case diagram — learn the notation for each, because drawing them correctly is where the marks are:

RelationshipNotationMeaning
AssociationSolid line, no arrowheadBasic communication between an actor and a use case.
IncludeDashed line, arrowhead pointing at the included use case, labelled <<include>>A mandatory part of the base use case — the base use case is incomplete without it, so it always runs. E.g. Login includes Authentication.
ExtendDashed line, arrowhead pointing at the base use case, labelled <<extend>>Optional additional functionality — only runs when certain criteria are met. E.g. Search Articles is extended by Help on Search.
Generalisation of an actorSolid line, solid arrowhead pointing at the ancestor actorOne actor inherits all the use cases of another, then adds its own. E.g. Registered User inherits from User.
Generalisation of a use caseSolid line, solid arrowhead pointing at the general use caseSpecialised versions of a general action. E.g. Card Payment, Cash Payment and Bank Transfer generalise to Make Payment.
💡
Include vs extend trips everyone up. Ask: does the base use case always need it? If yes → <<include>>, and the arrow points at the included (dependent) use case. If it only sometimes happens → <<extend>>, and the arrow points back at the base use case. The arrows point in opposite directions — examiners check this.

Worked Example

Use case questions in the question paper usually give you a scenario and either ask you to draw the diagram or complete/identify parts of one. Attempt this one yourself before expanding it.

Use Case Diagram — 2023 Question 3

Work through the scenario methodically:

  • Step 1 — identify the actors. Who (or what) interacts with the system from outside? Remember non-human actors — a server, a payment system, a database.
  • Step 2 — identify the use cases. Each individual action a user can perform gets its own ellipse, inside the system boundary.
  • Step 3 — add the relationships. Solid lines for associations; then check for anything that always happens as part of another action (<<include>>) or only sometimes happens (<<extend>>); finally look for actors or use cases that inherit from another (generalisation).

Test Yourself

Attempt each question first, then expand the marking instructions to check your answer.

Types of Feasibility — 2 marks

A software company is asked to develop a booking system for a chain of dental surgeries. The system must be ready before a new surgery opens in three months, and must store patient medical records.

Describe two types of feasibility the company should research before accepting the contract, with reference to this scenario. (2 marks)

Marking Instructions

Any two from, 1 mark each (must reference the scenario):

  • Time feasibility — can the system be developed and installed before the new surgery opens in three months?
  • Legal feasibility — patient medical records are sensitive personal data, so the system must comply with data-protection law (GDPR).
  • Technical feasibility — does the company have the tools/expertise to build a system of this kind?
  • Economic feasibility — can the system be produced within the surgery chain's budget / will the benefit justify the cost?
Include or Extend? — 2 marks

A cinema website allows customers to book tickets. Booking a ticket always requires the customer's card to be authorised by a payment provider. Customers may optionally add snacks to their booking.

State the relationship that should be used to connect each of the following to the Book Ticket use case, and justify each choice. (2 marks)

  • Authorise Card
  • Add Snacks
Marking Instructions
  • Authorise Card — <<include>>: it is mandatory — every booking must authorise the card, so Book Ticket is incomplete without it. (1 mark)
  • Add Snacks — <<extend>>: it is optional — it only runs when the customer chooses to add snacks. (1 mark)
Constraints — 1 mark

A requirements specification for a school app states: "The app must be developed in Python and run on the school's Windows 10 network."

State the term used to describe this part of the requirements specification. (1 mark)

Marking Instructions

Constraints — restrictions that apply to the development (development language and delivery platform). (1 mark)
